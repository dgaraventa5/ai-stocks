## 1. Market reaction, Nov 4–14, 2026

### Takeaway
The reaction came in three phases. On Nov 4, the broad market rallied, hyperscalers jumped, and AI power and neocloud names sold off. On Nov 5, AI and chip stocks crashed: the SOX fell about 12% (its worst day since March 2020), the S&P 500 fell about 4.4%, and the Nasdaq Composite fell about 5.5%, as the Democratic sweep and anti-data-center ballot results combined with weak chip earnings. From Nov 6 through Nov 13, markets partly rebounded; the S&P 500 and Nasdaq had their best week since August even as NVDA fell on its CEO's exit. The rotation the market attributed to the election ran out of "build-new" AI infrastructure (chips, merchant power, neoclouds) and into "already-powered" incumbents (hyperscalers).

### Inferences
- The election-attributable signal is clearest in two places: (a) VST's roughly 14% drop on the Texas governor result plus the KeyBanc downgrade, and (b) the Nov 4 hyperscaler pop alongside the neocloud and IPP drop. Together these show the market pricing a **"scarcity premium on existing powered capacity, discount on new-build optionality"** trade. This is the most useful framing for this watchlist.
- The Nov 5 SOX crash cannot be cleanly separated into election, earnings (QCOM/ARM), and crowded-positioning components. The equal-weight S&P falling only about 2.9% against SMH's −11.5% suggests a positioning unwind amplified by a political catalyst. It does not look like a re-pricing of federal policy, since federal AI law was not on the ballot.
- NVDA's week of Nov 9–13 is dominated by the CEO resignation. Any study of NVDA's "election reaction" must end on Nov 6.
- The Nov 4 "relief rally" and the Nov 5 crash show that the market first priced gridlock (good for the index) and then priced data-center politics (bad for the build-new chain). The two effects hit different layers.

## 2. Strategist and analyst views

### Takeaway
Sell-side consensus is that federal policy changes little in the near term, because divided government plus a presidential veto means tariffs and export controls stay with the executive branch. The real risk is state and local: data-center permitting, power tariffs for large loads, and tax abatements. Goldman and Morgan Stanley both say a national moratorium is unlikely and the AI capex cycle remains intact. UBS calls the selloff a "positioning reset." No post-election notes from Evercore ISI, Raymond James (Washington Policy), Strategas, Capital Alpha or Beacon Policy were found in public reporting, apart from one pre-election Evercore view.

  - Confidence: Medium.

### Inferences
- The sell-side split is not bull versus bear on AI. It is a split on **where** the risk sits. Federal risk (moratorium, AI liability, antitrust law) is low probability through 2027 because of the veto. State and local risk (permitting, large-load tariffs, tax abatements) is real and has already shown up at the ballot box. The investable read is that **geography and the contracted status of power/MW matter more than federal headlines.**
- Ratepayer protection is the one *bipartisan* channel: 417–3 in the House, and Hawley on the right. It is the policy most likely to become law in some form, and it targets who pays for grid upgrades, not whether data centers get built.

## 3. Historical precedent

### Takeaway
Base rates are favorable but rest on small samples. The S&P 500 has been higher 12 months after every midterm since WWII, and Stovall's Oct 31 to Oct 31 window averages about 16% with 100% hit rate. Whether divided government is better than unified government is disputed across sources. The one relevant tech-specific precedent, the June 2019 House antitrust probe, caused a sharp one-day selloff with little lasting fundamental impact. **2026 differs from those precedents in one important way:** the policy risk is concentrated in one sector's physical buildout (data centers and power), and that sector was crowded going into the vote.

### Cited Findings
- **Post-midterm 12-month returns:**
  - The S&P 500 rose in 32 of the last 38 post-midterm 12-month periods since 1874, averaging about 14% — [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/research/midterm-election-year-stock-market-history/).
  - Another series averages 12.4% over the following 12 months, and 17.5% for elections since 1980 — [U.S. Bank](https://www.usbank.com/investing/financial-perspectives/market-news/stock-market-performance-after-midterm-elections.html); [Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/2026-midterm-elections-and-stocks).
  - The S&P 500 has been higher a year after every midterm since WWII, as reported in 2018 — [Fox Business](https://foxbusiness.com/features/us-stocks-typically-ride-midterm-elections-to-solid-gains).
  - Confidence: Medium. Windows and data sets differ by source; do not average them together.
- **Seasonal pattern (CFRA, Sam Stovall):**
  - Q4 of a midterm year averages about +5.5% for the S&P 500, up 75% of the time after a positive Q3 — [Mitrade](https://www.mitrade.com/insights/news/live-news/article-3-2122651-20260929); [BeInCrypto](https://beincrypto.com/midterm-year-q4-gains-stovall-explains/).
  - Oct 31 of the midterm year to Oct 31 of the following year: about +16% price (about +20% total return), up 100% of the time — [MoneyShow](https://moneyshow.com/articles/dailyguru-57430).
  - Q2 and Q3 of the midterm year are the only back-to-back average declines in the 16-quarter presidential cycle — same sources.
  - Separately, Matt Cerminaro's analysis (not Stovall's): the S&P 500 averaged +15.5% from Election Day to June 30 of the following year since 1950 — [Yahoo/MarketWatch](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/wall-street-strategist-lays-simple-023700678.html).
  - Confidence: Medium.
- **Divided versus unified government:**
  - Morgan Stanley: about 23% average year-after return under divided government versus about 16% under full Democratic control — [Morgan Stanley](https://www.morganstanley.com/insights/articles/2026-us-midterm-elections-stock-market-impact).
  - Invesco calls "markets prefer divided government" a misconception — [Invesco](https://www.invesco.com/us/en/insights/midterm-elections-economic-growth.html).
  - CFRA's Stovall, cited by Kiplinger in 2018, has been quoted as saying gridlock is a minus — [Kiplinger](https://www.kiplinger.com/article/investing/t043-c007-s001-2018-midterm-elections-loom-over-the-stock-market.html).
  - E*TRADE warns not to read much into the small samples — [E*TRADE](https://us.etrade.com/knowledge/library/perspectives/daily-insights/short-term-midterm).
  - Confidence: Medium that the evidence is genuinely mixed.
- **2018 (Democratic House, Republican Senate, Trump):** equities "jumped sharply higher" the day after. The Dow rose 545 points on Nov 7, 2018 — [USA Today via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/usa-today-us-edition/20181108/281681140895187); [Fox Business](https://foxbusiness.com/features/us-stocks-typically-ride-midterm-elections-to-solid-gains). Confidence: High.
- **2019 House Judiciary antitrust probe (the closest Big Tech precedent):**
  - Launched as a bipartisan investigation into competition in digital markets on 2019-06-03 — [House Judiciary Democrats press release](https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/house-judiciary-committee-launches-bipartisan-investigation-into-competition-in-digital-markets); [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2019/06/03/house-judiciary-launches-antitrust-investigation-big-tech-giants).
  - Tech stocks fell that Monday ("FB, Amazon shares drop") and rebounded afterward — [Udayavani/PTI](https://www.udayavani.com/english-news/us-launches-antitrust-probe-into-big-tech-firms-fb-amazon-shares-drop); [Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/5348935/big-tech-antitrust-probe/).
  - The same day's move was compounded by reports that the FTC would take Facebook and the DOJ would take Google — [Washington Times/AP](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2019/jun/3/report-ftc-to-lead-possible-facebook-antitrust-pro/).
  - An analyst at the time: "the reaction vastly exceeds the potential harm to the company" — [Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/5348935/big-tech-antitrust-probe/).
  - Confidence: High on the event; Medium on the reaction description.

### Inferences
- The 2019 precedent suggests that a Democratic House investigation on its own (subpoenas, hearings, a final report, which came in October 2020) produced headline volatility. The lasting legal risk came later, from *agency* actions by DOJ and FTC, not from legislation; the 2020 report's bills largely did not pass. By analogy, House or Senate Democratic oversight of hyperscaler data-center deals, AI-chip exports or AI safety in 2027 is likely to drive headline risk rather than enacted constraints, as long as the White House can veto.
- The 2018 analogue is a warning on timing, not direction. The day-after rally in 2018 was followed by a deep Q4 2018 drawdown driven by Fed tightening and the trade war, not by Congress. In 2026 the first post-election week already showed a crash and a partial rebound. The favorable 12-month base rate does not protect against crowded-sector unwinds.
- The base rates describe the *index*. They say nothing about a crowded AI-infrastructure sub-sector facing local political backlash; there is no clean historical precedent for that. The closest rough analogues are utility and nuclear siting backlash in the 1970s–80s and the shale-fracking local bans of the 2010s, but no source in this session tested those.

### Gaps
- Not source-verified in this session, so the exact figures are not stated here: the size of the June 3, 2019 FB/GOOGL/AMZN/AAPL drops, the Q4 2018 S&P drawdown, and returns after the 2006 and 2010 midterms. A local yfinance pull would verify all of them.
- No sector-level (utilities, semis) post-midterm history was found; only index-level history.

They are **not** model scores and **not** trade recommendations. Probability labels reflect the sell-side and prediction-market reads in sections 2 and 4.

### Takeaway
The channel with the highest probability and the most direct impact is **state and local data-center and ratepayer rules**. It hits merchant power in Texas and PJM, neoclouds and bitcoin-miner hosts that need new interconnects, and DC construction and cooling contractors. ### Inferences
- **Clearest layer split:** the market rewarded *owned, energized* capacity (hyperscalers) and punished *contracted-future* capacity (neoclouds, IPPs with Texas or PJM merchant exposure, miners). For a watchlist that values Layer-9 capacity names on EV per secured MW, the risk is that **"secured" MW in jurisdictions with hostile new officials or ballot results may deserve a haircut**. The market has already applied an implicit discount. This is a research flag, not a methodology proposal.
- **Behind-the-meter power (BE, SEI, PSIX, BW, PUMP) is two-sided.** Grid-interconnect friction strengthens the on-site power case. But local moratoria block the facility itself, which would also block on-site generation. No market evidence on these names was found.
- **Regulated utilities are not a clean hedge.** Ratepayer protection could improve their cost recovery from large loads. The same voter anger over bills makes rate cases harder and threatens the data-center load-growth story built into utility capex plans (D in Loudoun, AEP in Ohio, SO in Georgia, where anti-DC candidates won statewide).
- **Semis (L4–L7) took the hardest price hit on Nov 5, but their direct policy channel is the weakest.** Export controls are executive-led, and moratoria are local. Their exposure is second-order, through hyperscaler and neocloud capex expectations, plus crowding. This matches Morningstar's note that a DC slowdown hurts industrials more than chip suppliers.
- **Layer 11 robotics and most of Layer 10 software have little direct midterm channel**, beyond tariffs (foreign component makers) and AI liability (TSLA FSD, AI underwriters).
